# Every Time Jesus Wept: He Only Cried 3 Times (Each Means Something)

**WORD COUNT:** 3,628
**ESTIMATED RUNTIME:** 24 minutes 11 seconds at 150 WPM
**TONE:** Reverent-conversational

---

The shortest verse in the entire Bible is two words.

You have probably heard it. You might have memorized it as a child because it was the easiest one to remember. "Jesus wept." John chapter eleven, verse thirty-five. Two words. Five syllables in English. Three in Greek.

And here is what you were probably told about those two words: See, Jesus was human too. He had emotions. He cried when his friend died. End of lesson.

But that is not the whole story.

The original Greek word hiding underneath your English translation is *edakrusen*. It comes from the root word *dakruo*. And *dakruo* does not mean what you think it means. It does not mean he broke down. It does not mean he lost control. It means he shed tears. Silently. Quietly. Tears rolling down his face — but no sound coming out of his mouth.

This is not the crying you do at a funeral when you cannot hold it together. This is something else entirely.

And it is only the first time.

In the entire New Testament, Jesus of Nazareth cries exactly three times. Three moments. Three sets of tears. And each one is recorded with a different Greek word — because each one means something completely different.

This is not a story about a number. This is not a story about a verse. This is not a story about a doctrine. This is a story about every time the God of the universe — the one who spoke galaxies into existence and held oceans in the palm of his hand — every time that God leaned into his own creation and wept.

And if you can understand why he cried each time, you will understand something about the heart of God that most people never see.

Let us go to Bethany.

The town sits about two miles east of Jerusalem on the southeastern slope of the Mount of Olives. Today it is called Al-Eizariya — named after Lazarus, the man who died here and came back. But on that day, it was just a small village. Dust roads. Stone houses. And inside one of those houses, a family was falling apart.

Mary and Martha had sent word to Jesus four days earlier. "The one you love is sick." That was the message. Not "Lazarus is sick." Not "your friend is sick." "The one you love." They knew Jesus would come. He had to come. Lazarus was not just a follower. He was the one Jesus ate dinner with. The one he laughed with. The one whose house he stayed in when Jerusalem got too dangerous.

But Jesus did not come.

Day one passed. Nothing. Day two. Nothing. Day three — in Jewish tradition, this was the last day the soul was believed to hover near the body. If Jesus was going to heal Lazarus, he had to arrive by day three. Day three came. Day three went. The sun set. And with it, every shred of hope Mary and Martha had been clutching disappeared into the Judean darkness.

Day four. In the hot Middle Eastern climate, decomposition was now certain. The body was wrapped in linen strips coated with myrrh and aloe — seventy-five pounds of embalming spices, John tells us, which tells you this family had money. They had given Lazarus the burial of a man they loved beyond measure. The stone was rolled across the tomb entrance. And the man who could have stopped all of this had stayed exactly where he was, two miles away.

When Jesus finally arrived, Martha ran to meet him at the edge of town. Mary stayed inside the house. If you know these two sisters from the gospels, this is exactly who they are. Martha — the one who cannot sit still, the one who has to do something with her grief. Mary — the one who collapses inward, who cannot move from the floor.

And you can hear the ache in Martha's voice — the almost-accusation she had been holding for four days. She had probably rehearsed this sentence a hundred times since her brother stopped breathing.

"Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died."

Mary said the exact same thing when she saw him. Same words. Same wound.

And then something happens that still confuses Bible scholars two thousand years later. Jesus sees Mary crying. He sees the villagers crying. The text says he was deeply moved in spirit and greatly troubled. The Greek word here is *enebrimēsato* — it does not just mean sad. It means he snorted with anger. Like a war horse before battle. He was furious. But not at Mary. Not at the mourners. He was furious at death itself. At what sin had done to his creation. At the enemy that had stolen his friend.

And then he wept.

*Edakrusen.* Silent tears. The God who will raise this dead man in approximately twelve seconds stops first. And cries.

Why? He knows the ending. He knows Lazarus is about to walk out of that tomb. He knows Mary's tears are about to turn to laughter. So why cry?

Because that is the first reason Jesus weeps. Not for himself. Not because he does not know what to do. He weeps because you are in pain — right now, in this moment — and even though he knows the good ending is coming, your present suffering matters to him. Your right-now tears are seen. Your right-now grief is shared.

The shortest verse in the Bible. Two words. And they are telling you this: the God of the universe is not in a hurry to get past your pain. He sits in it with you.

But the first reason Jesus weeps is not the only reason.

Fast forward several months. Maybe a year.

Jerusalem is spread out below Jesus like a white stone city on a hill. The Temple gleams in the afternoon sun — Herod's Temple, one of the wonders of the ancient world. Some of its foundation stones were forty feet long. Forty feet. The outer courtyard could hold a hundred thousand worshippers. The gold plates covering the exterior were so dazzling that the Roman historian Tacitus would later write that anyone looking at the Temple from the Mount of Olives had to shield their eyes, as if staring into the sun itself.

You have to understand — to a first-century Jewish person, Jerusalem was not just a city. It was the center of the universe. It was the place where God had chosen to put his name. The Temple was where heaven and earth met. Every Jew in the ancient world faced Jerusalem when they prayed. Every Jewish festival drew pilgrims to this city by the tens of thousands. If God had a home address on earth, this was it.

Jesus is riding a donkey down the Mount of Olives. It is what we now call Palm Sunday. The crowd is going insane. They are throwing palm branches at his feet — palm branches were a symbol of Jewish nationalism, the equivalent of waving your nation's flag at a political rally. They are ripping their cloaks off and laying them on the road, a gesture you only did for a conquering king returning from battle. They are shouting "Hosanna" at the top of their lungs — a Hebrew word, *hoshia na*, which literally means "save us now."

Save us from Rome. Save us from oppression. Save us from the taxes and the soldiers and the humiliation of being a conquered people in our own land.

They think he is finally doing what they have been waiting six hundred years for. They think the revolution is starting. They think this is their liberation day. You can feel the electricity in the air. You can hear the hope cracking in their voices.

And Jesus is weeping.

Not *dakruo* this time. A different Greek word entirely. *Eklausten.* From *klaio.* This is not silent tears. This is the loud, audible, shoulder-shaking crying you hear at a funeral in the Middle East. This is public grief. This is wailing.

He is crying out loud on a day when everyone around him is celebrating.

And look at what he says through the tears. Luke chapter nineteen, verse forty-two. "Would that you, even you, had known on this day the things that make for peace. But now they are hidden from your eyes."

Then he tells them exactly what is going to happen. Your enemies will build a barricade around you. They will surround you. They will crush you and your children. They will not leave one stone upon another in this city.

And thirty-seven years later — many people in this crowd are still alive for this — a Roman general named Titus surrounds Jerusalem with four legions. Sixty thousand soldiers. They build a siege wall. They cut off the food supply. The Jewish historian Josephus, who was there as an eyewitness, records what happened next. People ate their leather belts. They boiled their sandals. Mothers did unspeakable things to survive. By the time it was over, more than one million Jewish people were dead. The Temple that Jesus was looking at — the one covered in gold — was burned to the ground. Roman soldiers pried the gold plates off the stones, and to get every last flake of melted gold, they literally took the building apart stone by stone. Not one stone left upon another. Exactly what Jesus said.

This is why he was weeping. Not because he was powerless. Not because he did not know what would happen. He knew exactly what would happen. He could see it. He could feel it. He was watching one million of his own people march toward a cliff — and they would not listen. They wanted a warrior king. They got a weeping one instead. And that rejection sealed their fate.

So the first time Jesus wept, he wept because you were in pain. He saw Mary's tears, and even though he knew the resurrection was forty-five seconds away, he stopped. He entered her grief. He let it matter.

The second time, he wept because he could see the disaster you were heading toward — and you would not let him stop it. He saw Jerusalem's destruction thirty-seven years before a single Roman soldier marched toward the city. He saw the siege. He saw the starvation. He saw the Temple in flames. And the people he was weeping for were cheering him on, completely unaware that they were applauding their own rejection of the only person who could save them.

Do you see what connects these two moments? In both cases, Jesus knows the future. He knows the outcome. And in both cases, knowing the ending does not stop the tears. Because the tears are not about not knowing. The tears are about loving.

But there is a third time.

And this one is the hardest to look at.

The letter to the Hebrews was probably written by someone in Paul's circle — we are not sure exactly who. But the author knows something about Jesus that only shows up in one place in the entire New Testament. Hebrews chapter five, verse seven. Listen to this.

"In the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death."

With loud cries. The Greek says *kraugēs ischyras* — strong screaming. This is not quiet weeping. This is not public lament. This is a man on his face in the dirt, in the dark, screaming through tears so hard he cannot breathe.

This is Gethsemane.

The garden sits at the foot of the Mount of Olives — the same mountain he was riding down when he wept over Jerusalem. Some of the olive trees growing there today are old enough to have been alive when this happened. The name Gethsemane means "olive press" in Hebrew — a place where olives were crushed until the oil ran out.

Jesus came here after the Last Supper. After washing his disciples' feet — all twelve of them, including the one he knew would sell him for thirty pieces of silver before the sun came up. After breaking the bread and passing the cup and telling them his body was about to be broken and his blood was about to be poured out. After watching Judas walk out the door into the night.

He took Peter and James and John deeper into the garden. The three men who had seen him transfigured on the mountain. The three men who had seen his face shine like the sun. He chose them for this, too — to see him fall apart. And then Matthew's gospel says he began to be sorrowful and troubled. The Greek here is *perilypos* — surrounded by sorrow. Drowning in it. He said to them, "My soul is very sorrowful, even to death. Remain here and watch with me."

Even to death. He was not being poetic. He was telling them the truth — what I am about to feel is so heavy it could kill me right here in this garden before the soldiers ever arrive.

Then he went a little farther. About a stone's throw, Luke says. Close enough that they could hear him. Close enough that they would never forget the sound. And he fell on his face. Not on his knees — that is a posture of reverence. Not sitting with his head in his hands — that is a posture of exhaustion. On his face. The posture of a man who has been hit by something so massive he cannot physically remain upright.

And he begged. "Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me."

What was in the cup? In the Old Testament, the cup is almost always the cup of God's wrath. Isaiah calls it the cup of staggering. Jeremiah calls it the wine of wrath. Jesus is looking into a cup filled with every sin that has ever been committed — every murder, every betrayal, every act of cruelty, every secret you have ever carried — and he is being asked to drink it. All of it. Every drop.

The one man in human history who never did anything wrong. The one man who never deserved punishment. He is being asked to take the punishment for everything.

And he screams.

The God who spoke the universe into existence is on his face in the dirt, screaming into the night, tears mixing with the dust on his cheeks. And the Gospel of Luke adds a detail that makes you physically uncomfortable when you read it. Luke says his sweat became like great drops of blood falling to the ground.

There is a medical condition called hematidrosis. It is extremely rare. It happens when a person is under such extreme psychological stress that the capillaries around their sweat glands rupture. Blood mixes with sweat. It is a sign of a human being pushed past every limit.

This is the third reason Jesus weeps.

The first time — he weeps because you are suffering, and he enters into it with you. The second time — he weeps because he can see the destruction you are choosing, and you will not let him stop you. The third time — he weeps because he is the one about to suffer. For you.

Do you see the progression? In Bethany, he weeps because of what death has done to his friend. In Jerusalem, he weeps because of what judgment will do to his people. In Gethsemane, he weeps because of what the cross is about to do to him.

The tears get closer. They start with the pain of others. They move to the coming pain of the world. And they end with his own blood-streaked face in the dirt.

This is not a story about a weak man. This is not a story about a reluctant martyr. This is not a story about a teacher who got in over his head. This is a story about a God who chose to feel everything — your grief, your self-destruction, and the full weight of your sin — before he removed any of it.

He could have skipped straight to the resurrection. He could have bypassed the tears. He could have snapped his fingers at Lazarus's tomb without stopping to cry first. He could have ridden into Jerusalem without pausing on the hillside to weep. He could have gone to the cross without falling on his face in Gethsemane the night before.

But he did not.

Because the tears were not a detour on the way to the solution. The tears were part of the solution. Before he fixed anything, he felt everything.

And here is what that means for you.

The Roman historian Tacitus, writing in his Annals around AD 116, confirms that Jesus was executed under Pontius Pilate. He calls Christians a "mischievous superstition" and has no sympathy for them whatsoever. But he does not deny that the man existed. He does not deny that he died. The tears of Jesus are not a myth invented by later generations to make him seem relatable. They are recorded in historical documents by people who had no reason to make him look good.

The ancient world had gods who were powerful. Zeus with his thunderbolt. Mars with his sword. The gods of Greece and Rome were strong. They were impressive. They were terrifying.

But they never cried.

A god who weeps was a scandal to the ancient world. It is still a scandal today. We want a God who fixes things. A God who solves problems. A God who makes the pain go away. And Jesus does fix things. He does solve the ultimate problem. He does make the pain go away — in the end.

But first, he sits in the dust with you. First, he weeps over the city that is about to reject him. First, he falls on his face in the garden and screams.

So here are your three takeaways.

Takeaway one: Jesus wept at Lazarus's tomb — with silent tears — because your present suffering matters to him. Even when he knows the good ending is coming. Even when he knows he is about to fix it. He does not skip past your pain as if it is an inconvenience on the way to the solution. He enters it. The God you worship is not in a hurry to get through your grief. He sits in it. In it, not above it. With tears on his face, not with a stopwatch in his hand.

Takeaway two: Jesus wept over Jerusalem — with loud lament — because he could see what you are heading toward, and it breaks his heart. There are times in your life when you are running toward something that will destroy you. A relationship everyone can see is toxic except you. A decision you have already made but are still pretending to pray about. A pattern of sin you have dressed up in theological language so you do not have to call it what it is. And Jesus does not stand at a distance and shake his head. He weeps over you. He calls out to you. And if you will listen — even at the last moment, even from the back of a donkey on a road you have already chosen — he will save you from the destruction you cannot yet see.

Takeaway three: Jesus wept in Gethsemane — on his face, screaming, with blood in his sweat — because he was about to drink the cup of God's wrath. For you. Every sin you have ever committed. Every secret you hope no one finds out about. Every time you knew better and did it anyway. Jesus took the full weight of all of it. And it was so heavy, so crushing, so beyond what any human being could bear, that the God of the universe fell on his face in the dirt and screamed.

This is not a weak God. This is not an indifferent God. This is a God who chose to feel everything — your grief, your self-destruction, and the full eternal weight of your sin — before he removed any of it.

He did not have to do it. He chose to.

The shortest verse in the Bible is "Jesus wept." But those two words are not about weakness. They are about a love so deep that it chooses to feel everything before it fixes anything.

That is the God who sees you right now.
